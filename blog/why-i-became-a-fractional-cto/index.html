Why I Chose to Be a Fractional CTO Instead of Chasing a Full-Time Title

By Godwin Ubani · November 2026 · 6 min read

For a while, I assumed the goal was obvious: get good enough at building things, and eventually someone hands you a CTO title at a company with your name on the cap table. One company, one title, one roadmap. That's the story most of us grow up with.

I stopped chasing that story once I noticed what actually filled my week. I wasn't short on technical problems to solve — I was short on time, and surrounded by early-stage teams who had a product gap but not the budget, or the need, for a full-time technical co-founder. They needed someone who had already made the mistakes, not someone who would make them on the company's clock.

The problem with one seat

A full-time CTO role at a single company is a bet that one roadmap will stay interesting and one org chart will stay sane for years. Early-stage companies rarely offer either. Priorities shift every quarter, sometimes every sprint. If you're embedded in one seat, you absorb every one of those shifts personally — and you stop seeing patterns, because you only have one dataset.

Working fractionally across a handful of teams flips that. I see the same mistake show up in three different companies and recognize it on day one of the fourth. I see which architecture decisions actually mattered a year later and which ones were just bikeshedding dressed up as engineering rigor. That pattern recognition is the actual value a CTO brings — and it compounds faster across multiple rooms than inside one.

What fractional work actually looks like

It's not consulting in the vague sense of "giving advice." It's doing the work: reviewing architecture before it calcifies into technical debt, sitting in on hiring for the first engineering hires, making the build-vs-buy call before a team burns two months building something that should have been an API call, and being the person who says "no" to a feature so the team can ship the thing that actually matters.

The companies that get the most out of it treat it the way they'd treat any other senior hire — regular cadence, real access, real accountability. The ones that treat it as an occasional phone call don't get much value, and that's fair; the model only works with real involvement.

Why this fits how I build

I'm also building my own company, Korvanta, which means I know exactly what it feels like to sit on the other side of this relationship — to need senior technical judgment before I can afford to hire it full-time. That's the gap fractional work fills, for me and for the teams I work with. It's not a stepping stone to something else. It's the model that matches how most real technical work gets consumed at the early stage: in bursts, at the moments that matter, by someone who has already seen how the story ends if you get it wrong.

  • You get senior judgment at the moments that actually determine outcomes — architecture, hiring, build-vs-buy — not a diluted version of it spread across every day.
  • You avoid paying full-time cost for a role your company doesn't need full-time yet.
  • You get someone who has already seen the mistake you're about to make, in someone else's codebase.
