Building Korvanta

What Building Korvanta Taught Me About Cross-Border Trade Infrastructure

By Godwin Ubani · November 2026 · 7 min read

"Just build a payment app" is the kind of sentence that sounds reasonable until you actually try to move money between Africa and China. The idea behind Korvanta is simple to say out loud: make it easier for businesses trading between the two regions to pay, get paid, and trust that the other side will hold up their end. Building the thing underneath that sentence is where it gets hard.

The problem isn't the payment rail

When people hear "cross-border trade infrastructure," they assume the bottleneck is technical — which gateway to integrate, which API to call. In practice, the rails exist. What's missing is trust at the edges: a buyer in Lagos and a supplier in Guangzhou who have never met, operating under different banking systems, different regulatory regimes, and different defaults for what "proof of delivery" even means. The software has to encode trust that doesn't exist yet between the two parties, not just move numbers from one account to another.

That reframes the whole engineering problem. It's not "how do we process a transaction," it's "how do we make each side comfortable enough to initiate one." Every feature decision gets filtered through that — verification flows, status visibility, dispute handling — before it gets filtered through anything about throughput or latency.

Why we started with Nigeria

Building for "Africa" as a single market is a mistake I watched other teams make before I started Korvanta. Regulatory environments, banking infrastructure, and trade volumes vary enormously country to country. Starting narrow — with Nigeria specifically — meant we could get the compliance and banking relationships right for one jurisdiction instead of building a lowest-common-denominator product that worked adequately everywhere and well nowhere.

It also meant faster feedback. A narrower starting market gets you in front of real buyers and suppliers faster, which matters more than any architecture decision early on — you cannot design trust-building flows from a whiteboard, you have to watch where real transactions actually create friction.

What this means for how we build

On the technical side, that pushed us toward a few consistent choices: treat compliance and verification as core product surfaces, not backend checkboxes; make transaction status and documentation visible to both sides instead of locked inside one party's dashboard; and build for auditability from day one, because in cross-border trade, the question "can you prove this happened?" comes up far more often than "how fast was it?"

None of this is unique to trade finance — it's the same lesson that shows up anywhere software is trying to replace a handshake. The code is the easy part. Earning the right for two strangers to trust a system enough to move real money through it is the actual product.
